Common Pitfalls.

Q F D done once at kickoff and never revisited. A House of Quality built during concept selection reflects the customer requirements and competitive landscape as they existed at that moment. Requirements change as a program matures, a customer adds a requirement, a competitor releases a product that resets the benchmarking row, or a cost target tightens, and the engineering-characteristic rankings that were correct at kickoff can become stale well before the design freezes. Teams that treat Q F D as a one-time kickoff deliverable rather than a living document lose the ability to explain, later in the program, why a given subsystem received the reliability allocation and test rigor it did, and lose the early warning that a requirement's importance has shifted enough to justify revisiting that allocation.

P-diagrams drawn without engineering input from the team that will run the D F M E A. A P-diagram built by a single design engineer, or by a systems engineer working from a specification document without the component-level design and manufacturing engineers in the room, systematically under-populates the noise-factor list, exactly the failure mode narrowly avoided in the gearbox example above by including the D F M E A team early. The people who understand how a part is actually manufactured, installed, and maintained in the field are the people most likely to know which noise factors matter, and they are frequently not the people who default to owning the P-diagram exercise. Because the D F M E A (Chapters 8 to 9) will inherit this same noise-factor list as its starting point for cause analysis, a thin P-diagram produces a thin D F M E A downstream, compounding the gap rather than catching it.

Checklists substituting for engineering judgment. As O'Connor warns, a checklist item marked complete is not the same as the underlying question having been genuinely thought through. A checklist with vague, generic items (has reliability been considered?) gives a false sense of verification coverage precisely because every item can be checked off without engaging with the specific design. The fix is the same one given in Section 4.3: keep checklist items specific enough that answering them requires looking at the actual drawing or the actual noise-factor list, not just recalling that the topic was mentioned in a meeting.

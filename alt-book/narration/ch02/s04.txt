Section 2.4. Where the Number Actually Comes From: Usage Data, Not Guesswork.

Before any of the conversions in section 2.3 mean anything, someone has to answer a prior question: what usage level is this requirement even being written against? The life of the vehicle is not a number. It becomes one only when it's grounded in how the population of actual customers uses the product, and the right way to do that is with real usage-distribution data, not a single average assumption.

A documented industry case makes this concrete. A vehicle manufacturer analyzed dealership repair records across several European markets, converting sales-to-repair distance data into annual driving-distance figures. The result fit a lognormal distribution (mu equals 3.867, sigma equals 0.603): an average annual driving distance of 20,600 kilometres, a median of 17,200 kilometres, and a 95th-percentile driver at 46,000 kilometres per year. The manufacturer then built the engine reliability specification for its Tier 2 supplier around the 95th-percentile figure, not the average, because a requirement written to the average driver leaves the top 5 percent of the customer population, not a small number of people, across a large production volume, under-specified from day one. This is the same percentile-selection logic Chapter 1's opening statistic depends on, and it's the same logic Part II will return to in far more depth when we build full duty cycles rather than single design-life numbers.
